# Nature Dependency Screening

A practical guide for farmers, small businesses, and any operation with a physical supply chain who wants to understand how their business depends on the natural world, and what happens when those natural systems are stressed or lost.

---

## Why Nature Dependencies Matter to a Business

A strawberry farm depends on pollinators to set fruit. A brewery depends on consistent water supply and grain quality from predictable rainfall. A timber operation depends on healthy soil biology and watershed function. A seafood company depends on healthy fisheries and coastal ecosystems.

These are not abstract environmental concerns. They are operational dependencies. When the ecosystem service fails, the business either fails with it or absorbs the cost elsewhere. Understanding your dependencies is the first step toward managing the risk.

---

## The Five Categories of Ecosystem Services

Ecosystem services are the benefits that functioning natural systems provide to people and economies. They are conventionally grouped into five categories:

### 1. Provisioning Services

The direct outputs of ecosystems that you can use or sell.

Examples:
- Fresh water from rivers, aquifers, and rainfall
- Food: crops, livestock, fish, wild-harvested products
- Fiber: timber, cotton, wool
- Genetic resources: crop wild relatives used in plant breeding
- Medicinal plants and compounds

**Business relevance**: Any company that uses water, grows food, harvests timber, or relies on raw biological materials is directly dependent on provisioning services.

### 2. Regulating Services

The benefits ecosystems provide by controlling natural processes, often invisible until they are lost.

Examples:
- Climate regulation: forests sequester carbon and moderate local temperatures
- Water purification: wetlands and soil filter pollutants from water
- Flood regulation: intact floodplains and wetlands buffer flood peaks
- Pollination: wild bees and other insects enable crop reproduction
- Pest and disease control: predators and habitat diversity limit pest outbreaks
- Erosion control: vegetation and root systems hold soil in place
- Air quality: trees filter particulates

**Business relevance**: A farm that loses natural pest control faces higher pesticide costs. A city whose upstream wetlands are drained faces higher water treatment costs. A coastal business whose mangroves are removed loses natural storm surge protection.

### 3. Cultural Services

Non-material benefits from ecosystems, including recreation, tourism, spiritual and cultural value.

Examples:
- Nature-based tourism and recreation (hiking, fishing, diving, birdwatching)
- Cultural identity and heritage associated with landscapes
- Spiritual and religious value of natural places
- Aesthetic value that supports property prices and quality of life

**Business relevance**: Any business in tourism, hospitality, outdoor recreation, or real estate is directly exposed. Degraded landscapes have lower visitor appeal and lower property value.

### 4. Supporting Services

The foundational processes that underpin all other ecosystem services. These are not consumed directly, but without them everything else fails.

Examples:
- Soil formation: the biological and physical processes that create productive topsoil over centuries
- Nutrient cycling: decomposition and the recycling of nitrogen, phosphorus, and carbon through soil and water
- Primary production: photosynthesis, the base of all food chains
- Habitat provision: the structural complexity that allows biodiversity to persist

**Business relevance**: Soil formation is the foundational supporting service for all agriculture. Topsoil takes roughly 500 years to form naturally per inch. Modern intensive farming can lose that inch in decades. Once degraded, it is effectively a non-renewable resource on human timescales.

### 5. Informational Services

The ability of ecosystems to provide signals, patterns, and data that help us make decisions.

Examples:
- Biodiversity as an indicator of ecosystem health
- Indicator species that signal environmental change (amphibians are highly sensitive to pollution and serve as early warning systems)
- Natural patterns that inspire design and engineering
- Genetic diversity as a resource for future adaptation and crop breeding

## Identifying Your Business Dependencies: The ENCORE Methodology

ENCORE (Exploring Natural Capital Opportunities, Risks and Exposure) is a free tool developed by UNEP-WCMC and the Natural Capital Finance Alliance specifically to help businesses identify which ecosystem services they depend on and impact.

**Where**: encore.naturalcapital.finance, free, no account required.

**How it works**: You select your production process or industry (from a menu covering roughly 160 production processes), and ENCORE maps which ecosystem services you depend on and which you impact. It rates the sensitivity of your business to disruption of each service.

**Example, wheat farming**: ENCORE identifies dependencies on water supply, soil quality, climate regulation, and pollination. It flags water supply as a high sensitivity dependency, meaning significant business disruption would result from reduced water availability.

**Example, hotel and tourism**: ENCORE identifies dependencies on climate regulation, water supply, and aesthetic landscape quality. It flags landscape quality as a key driver of tourism revenue.

You do not need a consultant to use ENCORE. Budget two hours, select the production processes that apply to your business, and read the dependency map it produces.

## Water Risk Screening: WRI Aqueduct

Water is the most commonly underestimated physical resource risk for businesses that use it. WRI Aqueduct is a free tool that maps water risk at the watershed level globally.

**Where**: aqueduct.wri.org, free, no account required.

### What Aqueduct Measures

**Baseline Water Stress**: The ratio of water withdrawals to available renewable water in a watershed. High stress means many users are competing for limited supply. A score of 4 to 5 (on a 0 to 5 scale) indicates extremely high stress, meaning more than 80% of available water is already being withdrawn. Agriculture typically accounts for 70% of global freshwater withdrawal.

**Interannual Variability**: How much does water supply vary from year to year in this watershed? High variability means even a normally water-adequate location can face sudden shortages in drought years. Business planning that assumes average conditions can fail badly in low-water years.

**Drought Risk**: A combined measure of the probability and intensity of droughts at your location. Distinct from baseline stress, since a region can have low baseline stress but high drought risk if rainfall is highly variable.

**Coastal Eutrophication Potential**: Nutrient pollution (nitrogen and phosphorus) that contributes to oxygen-depleted dead zones in coastal and freshwater systems. Important for agricultural operations near waterways, and for food companies sourcing from areas with intensive agriculture.

**Groundwater Table Decline**: Measures depletion of aquifers over time. Many of the most productive agricultural regions draw on aquifers being depleted faster than they recharge, including the Ogallala Aquifer in the US Great Plains, the Central Valley aquifer in California, and aquifers in northern India and Pakistan. This is a slow-moving but existential risk for operations depending on groundwater.

### How to Read the Tool

1. Go to aqueduct.wri.org
2. Enter your location or zoom to your watershed on the map
3. Select Water Risk and then the indicators relevant to your business
4. The color scale runs from green (low risk) to dark red (extremely high risk)
5. Click any location for a detailed breakdown of scores and their confidence levels

**Practical interpretation**: A baseline water stress score above 3 (high stress) warrants proactive investigation. Understand your water source, efficiency of use, and what happens to your operation if water costs double or availability declines 30%.

## Deforestation Risk in Supply Chains

If your business sources commodities linked to deforestation, you face regulatory, reputational, and supply disruption risks that are growing rapidly.

### The Highest-Risk Commodities

Based on deforestation research from Forest Trends, Chain Reaction Research, and the Global Canopy Programme:

1. **Beef and leather**: Cattle ranching drives the majority of Amazon deforestation. Brazil supplies roughly 20% of global beef exports. Leather supply chains carry the same risk profile.
2. **Soy**: Primarily used as animal feed. Brazil and Argentina account for about 55% of global soy exports. Deforestation linked to soy expansion is highest in the Cerrado (Brazilian savanna) but also occurs in the Amazon.
3. **Palm oil**: The most efficient vegetable oil crop by yield. Indonesia and Malaysia account for 85% or more of global supply. Expansion has driven significant deforestation and peatland drainage. Draining peatlands releases large quantities of stored carbon.
4. **Cocoa**: Concentrated in West Africa, where Ivory Coast and Ghana together supply about 60% of global cocoa. Deforestation within protected areas has been documented in multiple major brands supply chains.
5. **Timber and paper**: Risk depends on sourcing region. FSC and PEFC certification schemes indicate verified legal and sustainable sourcing.

### Tools for Screening Deforestation Risk

**Global Forest Watch (globalforestwatch.org)**: Free, interactive map of forest cover change globally, updated annually using satellite data. You can view deforestation alerts down to 30-meter resolution. GFW also has a Commodities module for company-level screening.

**Trase (trase.earth)**: Tracks commodity supply chains from production regions to trading companies to importing countries. Covers soy, beef, palm oil, and coffee. Trase can help you identify whether your suppliers sourcing area is in a high-deforestation region.

**The EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR)**: As of 2024, large companies selling products linked to seven key commodities (cattle, cocoa, coffee, palm oil, soy, wood, rubber) in the EU market must demonstrate due diligence that the product was not produced on land deforested after December 31, 2020. Smaller operators have an additional 18-month transition period. If your business sells into the EU or sources from supply chains that do, this is now a compliance issue, not just a reputational one.

## Soil Health Basics

Soil is the most underappreciated business asset in agriculture. A teaspoon of healthy topsoil contains roughly 1 billion bacteria, hundreds of meters of fungal filaments, and thousands of other organisms. These organisms drive nutrient cycling, disease suppression, water retention, and root health.

### What Makes Soil Healthy

**Organic matter content**: Healthy agricultural soils typically contain 3 to 5% organic matter. Each 1% increase in organic matter allows soil to hold approximately 20,000 additional gallons of water per acre, which is a direct drought resilience benefit. Many intensively farmed soils in the US have declined to 1 to 2% organic matter over decades of tillage and bare-soil practices.

**Soil structure and compaction**: Healthy soil has a porous structure that allows roots to penetrate, water to infiltrate, and air to reach soil organisms. Heavy equipment, tillage, and repeated traffic compact soil, reducing porosity. Compacted soil drains poorly, increases surface runoff, and reduces crop yields. Root growth is typically restricted when penetration resistance exceeds 300 psi at 6 inches depth.

**Biological activity**: The soil food web, which includes bacteria, fungi, nematodes, earthworms, and other organisms, drives nutrient cycling. Healthy biological activity can supply 30 to 50% or more of crop nitrogen needs through decomposition and fixation. Mycorrhizal fungi extend plant root systems dramatically, improving phosphorus uptake and drought resistance.

### Key Indicators to Monitor

- **Organic matter percentage**: Soil test from your local cooperative extension service lab, typically $20 to $50
- **Infiltration rate**: Time a measured amount of water to infiltrate 6 inches into the soil. Healthy soil should absorb 1 inch per hour or more.
- **Earthworm count**: In one square foot of soil to 6 inches depth, 10 or more earthworms indicates healthy biology
- **Compaction reading**: Penetrometer resistance above 300 psi at 6 inches depth typically indicates growth-limiting compaction

### Why Soil Health Matters Beyond Yield

Improving soil health reduces fertilizer input costs through better nutrient cycling. Soils with higher organic matter retain water longer, reducing irrigation demand. Healthy soils also reduce nutrient runoff into waterways, which matters for regulatory compliance and downstream water quality. Finally, soil carbon sequestration is now a tradeable commodity. See the Payment for Ecosystem Services section.

## Biodiversity and Habitat: IBAT

IBAT (Integrated Biodiversity Assessment Tool) is the standard professional tool for understanding proximity of business operations to protected areas and biodiversity hotspots.

**Where**: ibat-alliance.org. Subscription-based, but free access is available to small businesses and NGOs through an application process. Some single-location reports can be purchased for a one-time fee.

### What IBAT Shows

**IUCN Red List species**: The global database of threatened and endangered species. IBAT can show which threatened species are present at or near your operation based on known ranges.

**WDPA Protected Areas**: The World Database on Protected Areas covers national parks, wildlife reserves, marine protected areas, and other legally designated conservation areas. IBAT shows proximity to these areas and their protection category.

**Key Biodiversity Areas (KBAs)**: Sites of global significance for biodiversity, identified based on species concentrations, endemism, and ecological integrity. Operating near a KBA is increasingly flagged by lenders, insurers, and major buyers as a risk factor.

**A free starting point**: If you cannot access IBAT, Protected Planet (protectedplanet.net) allows free searching of protected areas near any location. This covers the WDPA database but not the full species and KBA data that IBAT provides.

### Why This Matters Financially

**Regulatory risk**: Increasing numbers of national regulations and international agreements, including the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework and the EU Biodiversity Strategy, are creating obligations to avoid, mitigate, or offset impacts on protected or high-value biodiversity areas.

**Lender scrutiny**: Financial institutions are increasingly asking borrowers to screen physical assets against biodiversity sensitivity. The Equator Principles (which govern project finance from major international banks) require IBAT-level screening for projects above certain size thresholds.

**Buyer requirements**: Major food companies and retailers increasingly require suppliers to demonstrate that operations do not negatively impact protected areas or KBAs.

## Pollinator Dependency

Roughly 75% of the world's flowering plants depend on animal pollination. For crops, 35% of global food production by volume depends on pollinators, worth an estimated $235 to $577 billion annually according to IPBES (Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services).

### Which Crops Depend Most on Pollination

**Highly dependent**: yield falls 90% or more without pollinators:
- Almonds, nearly 100% dependent on honey bee pollination
- Cucumbers, melons, squash, pumpkins
- Blueberries, raspberries, strawberries

**Moderately dependent**: yield declines substantially but some production remains possible:
- Apples, pears, cherries, peaches
- Coffee
- Sunflowers, canola (oilseed rape)

**Low or not dependent**: wind-pollinated or self-pollinating:
- Wheat, corn, rice, oats (wind-pollinated grains)
- Tomatoes (can self-pollinate but benefit from buzz pollination)

### Colony Collapse Disorder and Wild Bees

Colony Collapse Disorder has reduced managed honey bee colonies in the US by roughly 30% since 2006. Many large-scale crop producers rent honey bee colonies for pollination, and costs and availability fluctuate with colony health. Wild bee diversity, which is more resilient than managed colonies for many crops, has also declined in parallel with habitat loss and pesticide exposure.

### What Farms Can Do

- **Establish hedgerows and flower strips**: A 5 to 10 meter flower strip along field margins planted with native wildflowers can significantly increase wild bee populations. Cost is low; the main input is seeding and allowing margins to grow.
- **Reduce or eliminate neonicotinoid insecticides**: These systemic insecticides are highly toxic to bees and persist in soil and pollen. Even where legal, they are a major risk factor for pollinator health on and around your farm.
- **Maintain rough, unmowed areas**: Many solitary bee species nest in bare soil or hollow stems. Leaving unmowed margins and brush piles supports wild pollinator populations at very low cost.
- **Time pesticide applications**: Spray at dawn or dusk when bees are least active. Never spray flowering crops while in bloom.

## The TNFD LEAP Approach Without Jargon

The Taskforce on Nature-related Financial Disclosures (TNFD) has developed a framework for how businesses should assess and disclose their nature dependencies and impacts. Their practical assessment method is called LEAP: Locate, Evaluate, Assess, Prepare.

You do not need to be a large company or hire a consultant to apply this thinking to your business.

### Locate: Where Are Your Activities?

List every location where your business has operations, sources materials, or disposes of waste. For a farm, this is straightforward. For a business with a supply chain, this includes where key inputs come from.

For each location, characterize the nature setting: What watershed are you in? What is the water stress level? Are there protected areas nearby? What dominant ecosystem type is present?

### Evaluate: What Do You Depend On and What Do You Impact?

Use ENCORE to map which ecosystem services you depend on. Then assess the condition of those services. Is the local watershed under stress? Is soil health declining on your land? Is the source forest for your timber input stable or shrinking?

Impacts are the flip side. Does your operation affect ecosystem services? Nutrient runoff from fields, water extraction from a stressed aquifer, habitat clearing for expansion are all impacts that affect other users and the ecosystem ability to provide services to your own future operations.

### Assess: What Are the Risks and Opportunities?

Convert your dependencies and impacts into business terms:
- If water availability declines 30% in this watershed, what happens to your operating costs or production capacity?
- If deforestation in your supply chain creates regulatory non-compliance, what is the cost of re-sourcing?
- If pollinators decline further, what is the yield impact on your orchard or field crop?
- On the opportunity side: improved soil health reduces fertilizer costs; ecosystem restoration on marginal land can generate income through biodiversity credits or carbon payments.

### Prepare: What Do You Act On?

For most small businesses, formal disclosure is not yet required. But the preparation step is valuable for your own planning:
- What actions reduce your nature-related risks?
- What monitoring tells you if conditions are changing?
- If a bank, investor, or major buyer asks about your nature risk, what can you say?

## Financial Materiality: How Nature Risk Connects to Your Bottom Line

Nature risk materializes in business through three pathways:

**Supply chain disruption**: If a key input, such as water, pollinators, stable climate, or healthy fish stocks, becomes unavailable or significantly more expensive, supply chain disruption follows. Businesses that have screened and managed these dependencies absorb shocks better than those operating blind.

**Regulatory risk**: The EU Deforestation Regulation, TNFD disclosure requirements phasing in across multiple jurisdictions, and expanding mandatory environmental due diligence laws are creating new compliance requirements. Businesses unaware of their supply chain nature impacts face non-compliance costs and potential market access restrictions.

**Reputational risk**: Major food companies, retailers, and brands are increasingly requiring suppliers to demonstrate responsible sourcing, free from deforestation, responsible water use, and responsible land management. Failure to demonstrate these standards is increasingly a reason to be removed from supply chains.

## Payment for Ecosystem Services and Nature-Based Income

Landowners and farmers have access to income streams that reward responsible stewardship of natural assets. These are real income opportunities, not theoretical.

**Conservation easements**: A legal agreement that restricts development on your land in exchange for an upfront payment from a land trust or government agency, or a charitable tax deduction. The IRS allows a deduction for the value of the development rights surrendered. Conservation easements can generate significant one-time payments for agricultural land with wetlands, habitat corridors, or water quality significance.

**Biodiversity credits and habitat banking**: In jurisdictions with biodiversity net gain requirements (England made this mandatory as of 2024 under the Environment Act), developers must offset their habitat impacts. Landowners who create or restore habitat can sell units into this market. Prices vary by habitat type and location.

**Carbon sequestration payments**: Farmers and land managers in the US can participate in voluntary carbon markets by adopting practices that increase soil organic carbon, such as cover crops, reduced tillage, and compost application. Payments range from $10 to $50 per metric ton of CO2 sequestered depending on the program and verification methodology. See American Carbon Registry, Climate Action Reserve, and Soil and Water Outcomes Fund for US programs.

**USDA conservation programs**: The Conservation Reserve Program (CRP), Conservation Stewardship Program (CSP), and Environmental Quality Incentives Program (EQIP) pay US farmers for conservation practices including cover crops, wetland restoration, riparian buffers, and pollinator habitat. Your local Farm Service Agency office has current payment rates.

**Wetland mitigation banking**: If your property contains restorable wetlands, wetland mitigation banks create compensatory mitigation credits sold to developers who impact wetlands elsewhere. This is a regulated market under the Clean Water Act Section 404 permitting process.

## Quick Reference Checklist

### Step 1: Know your dependencies
- [ ] Use ENCORE (encore.naturalcapital.finance) to map which ecosystem services your business depends on, budget 2 hours
- [ ] Identify your top three dependency risks based on the output

### Step 2: Screen water risk
- [ ] Enter your primary location in WRI Aqueduct (aqueduct.wri.org)
- [ ] Note baseline water stress, interannual variability, and drought risk scores
- [ ] If any score is above 3 (high), investigate your water source and plan for a 30% availability reduction scenario

### Step 3: Screen biodiversity risk if near sensitive areas
- [ ] Check proximity to protected areas using Protected Planet (protectedplanet.net, free) or IBAT
- [ ] Identify whether any Key Biodiversity Areas are within 10 km of your operations

### Step 4: Screen deforestation risk if relevant to your supply chain
- [ ] Identify which of the seven high-risk commodities (beef, soy, palm oil, cocoa, timber, rubber, coffee) appear in your inputs
- [ ] Use Global Forest Watch to check deforestation trends in your sourcing regions
- [ ] If you sell into the EU: review EUDR compliance requirements now

### Step 5: Assess soil health (agricultural operations)
- [ ] Get a basic soil test including organic matter percentage from your cooperative extension service
- [ ] Measure infiltration rate at three locations on your land
- [ ] Identify the one or two practices most likely to improve organic matter on your specific operation

### Step 6: Evaluate income opportunities
- [ ] Contact your local land trust about conservation easements if you own farmland with natural features
- [ ] Inquire with your Farm Service Agency office about CRP, EQIP, and CSP enrollment
- [ ] Investigate soil carbon programs if you are adopting cover crops or reduced tillage
