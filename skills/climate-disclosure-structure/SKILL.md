# Climate Disclosure Structure

A practical guide for small to medium businesses navigating what they need to disclose about climate risk, what the frameworks actually require in plain language, and what the common mistakes look like.

---

## Who Needs to Disclose What Right Now

The short answer is: it depends on where you operate, who your investors are, and how large your company is. Here is the actual current picture.

### EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD)

CSRD replaces the Non-Financial Reporting Directive (NFRD) and significantly expands which companies must report.

**Who is in scope**:
- **Wave 1 (fiscal year 2024, reports in 2025)**: Large public-interest entities already reporting under NFRD — typically listed companies and large financial institutions with 500+ employees
- **Wave 2 (fiscal year 2025, reports in 2026)**: Large companies meeting two of three criteria: 250+ employees, EUR 40 million or more in net turnover, EUR 20 million or more in total assets
- **Wave 3 (fiscal year 2026, reports in 2027)**: Listed SMEs, small non-complex credit institutions, and captive insurance companies
- **Wave 4 (from 2028)**: Non-EU companies with substantial EU activity (EUR 150 million net turnover in EU, with at least one EU subsidiary or branch)

**What CSRD requires**: Reporting under the European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS). Climate-specific standards are ESRS E1 (climate change). This covers climate-related risks and opportunities, greenhouse gas emissions (all three scopes), targets, and transition plans.

**Double materiality**: A distinctive feature of CSRD. You must assess both how climate affects your company (financial materiality) and how your company affects the climate (impact materiality). Both directions are required.

### US SEC Climate Disclosure Rules

The SEC adopted climate disclosure rules in March 2024 (though legal challenges are ongoing as of 2025). The rules apply to US-listed companies.

**Key requirements for large accelerated filers and accelerated filers**:
- Material climate-related risks and how they affect strategy
- Governance of climate risk (board and management oversight)
- Scenario analysis (if used in risk management)
- Scope 1 and Scope 2 emissions (with assurance for large accelerated filers)
- Material Scope 3 emissions (if material or if targets include Scope 3)
- Climate-related targets and progress

**Smaller reporting companies**: More limited requirements, longer timelines.

**Note on legal status**: The SEC rules faced immediate legal challenges. Check current status at sec.gov, as implementation timelines may have shifted.

### UK TCFD Requirements

The UK has mandated climate disclosure aligned with the Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD) recommendations for:
- UK-listed companies (in-scope since 2021 for premium listed companies)
- Large UK-registered companies (250+ employees and either GBP 500 million turnover or GBP 500 million balance sheet, since 2022)
- UK-regulated financial institutions

**What is required**: Disclosure against the four TCFD pillars (see below) in the Annual Report.

### If You Are None of the Above

If your company does not yet meet mandatory thresholds, you are still affected in two ways:

1. **Supply chain pressure**: If you are a supplier to a large company subject to CSRD or SEC rules, they will need your emissions data for their Scope 3 reporting. Expect questionnaires.

2. **Bank and lender requirements**: Many banks are now asking business loan applicants about climate risk and greenhouse gas emissions, particularly for larger commercial loans. The questions are increasingly standardized.

## TCFD Four Pillars in Plain Language

The Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD) is the foundation for most major climate disclosure frameworks. Understanding its four pillars unlocks every other framework.

### Pillar 1: Governance

**What it asks**: Who in your organization owns climate risk, and how does leadership engage with it?

**In plain language**: Does your board discuss climate risk at least annually? Is there a committee or executive responsible? What is their mandate and how do they report to the board?

**What poor governance disclosure looks like**: "Our board is responsible for sustainability." That is a statement of accountability that says nothing about process or engagement.

**What good governance disclosure looks like**: "The Audit and Risk Committee receives a climate risk briefing quarterly. The CEO reports to the board on climate-related capital allocation decisions. The Sustainability Director has a standing agenda item at every board meeting."

### Pillar 2: Strategy

**What it asks**: How does climate change affect your business model, strategy, and financial planning — now and in the future?

**In plain language**: What are the physical and transition risks that could affect your revenue, costs, or assets? What opportunities might climate change create? How does this affect your long-term strategy?

**Physical risk examples**: A food manufacturer whose key crop growing regions face increasing drought. A coastal hotel whose property is increasingly exposed to storm surge.

**Transition risk examples**: A building materials company that will face a carbon price on its cement production. An automaker whose combustion engine technology faces regulatory phase-out.

**Opportunity examples**: A heat pump manufacturer whose market is growing rapidly due to electrification policy. A sustainability consulting firm with expanding demand.

### Pillar 3: Risk Management

**What it asks**: How do you identify, assess, and manage climate-related risks?

**In plain language**: Is climate risk part of your enterprise risk management process? How do you screen for physical and transition risks? Who is responsible for ongoing monitoring?

**What this looks like in practice**: A company might screen its major facilities against WRI Aqueduct for water risk, model the impact of a carbon price on its input costs, and review physical risk annually as part of its operational risk process.

### Pillar 4: Metrics and Targets

**What it asks**: What do you measure to track your exposure to climate risk, and what commitments have you made?

**In plain language**: How much does your company emit (greenhouse gas inventory)? Have you set targets for reducing emissions? Are you tracking progress?

## Physical Risk vs. Transition Risk

These two categories of risk appear in every major framework. Understanding them concretely matters.

### Physical Risk

Physical risks are direct impacts of a changing climate on your business operations, supply chain, and assets.

**Acute physical risks**: Sudden, event-driven impacts.
- A hurricane damages your facility or interrupts your supply chain
- Extreme heat forces temporary shutdown of outdoor operations
- Flooding of a key supplier disrupts your production

**Chronic physical risks**: Gradual shifts in conditions that affect your business over time.
- Declining water availability in a water-stressed region where you operate
- Increasing average temperatures that raise cooling costs or reduce agricultural yields
- Sea level rise that threatens coastal facilities or makes them uninsurable

**How to identify your physical risks**: Map your locations and supply chain against physical climate data. The tools are free: WRI Aqueduct for water, NOAA and First Street Foundation for flood, Jupiter Intelligence or Four Twenty Seven (now Moody Analytics) for composite physical risk (subscription-based). For an initial free screen, the IPCC regional projections (atlas.ipcc.ch) give you a sense of direction for temperature, precipitation, and extreme event trends in your key geographies.

### Transition Risk

Transition risks arise from the economic and policy changes associated with moving to a lower-carbon economy.

**Policy and legal risks**:
- Carbon pricing: A price on greenhouse gas emissions that raises your operating costs (EU ETS already prices carbon for covered industries; several other jurisdictions are implementing or expanding carbon prices)
- Regulatory requirements: Building codes mandating efficiency standards; vehicle fleet emission standards; deforestation due diligence regulations

**Technology risk**:
- Obsolescence of existing high-carbon technologies (internal combustion engine vehicles, gas boilers)
- Higher cost of capital for assets associated with high emissions as lenders price climate risk

**Market risk**:
- Changing customer preferences toward lower-carbon products
- Buyers building climate criteria into procurement

**Reputational risk**:
- Consumer or investor backlash associated with high emissions or lack of credible disclosure

**How to assess transition risk**: Model the financial impact of a carbon price on your business (even if not currently in your jurisdiction, international competitiveness and supply chain pricing effects can reach you). Identify which of your products or assets have high-carbon substitutes that are cost-competitive now or will be within 5 to 10 years.

## Scenario Analysis: A Simple Approach

Every major framework recommends or requires scenario analysis, but it sounds more complex than it needs to be.

### What Scenario Analysis Means

Scenario analysis asks: how would your business look under different possible futures? It is not a prediction. It is a stress test.

For climate, the two scenarios most frameworks recommend you model are:

**Scenario A: 1.5 degree C, orderly transition**
A world where governments and markets successfully limit warming to 1.5 degrees C through strong carbon pricing, rapid technology deployment, and behavioral change. Key features for business planning: high carbon prices (potentially USD 100 to 300 per ton by 2030), rapid electrification of transport and heat, significant renewable energy expansion, possible stranded assets in fossil fuel-adjacent industries.

**Scenario B: 3 to 4 degree C, disorderly or insufficient action**
A world where climate policy is fragmented and insufficient, leading to physical climate impacts significantly more severe than today. Key features: more frequent and intense extreme weather events, sea level rise accelerating, water stress increasing in already-stressed regions, agricultural productivity impacts in tropical regions.

### A Simple Scenario Analysis You Can Do Without Consultants

For each scenario, answer three questions for your business:

1. What are the top three risks for your business under this scenario?
2. What are the financial impacts if those risks materialize? (Order of magnitude is fine, you do not need precise figures.)
3. What strategic choices would you make differently if you expected this scenario to play out?

Write it down. Review it with leadership. Update it annually. That is a defensible, credible scenario analysis for a small or medium business.

The scenario analysis does not need to be a 50-page consultant report. It needs to be honest, specific to your business, and show that you have actually thought about how climate futures affect your strategy.

## Materiality Assessment

A materiality assessment identifies which climate topics are most significant for your specific business. You need this because you cannot disclose everything, and what matters differs by sector and geography.

### Financial Materiality

A topic is financially material if it could reasonably affect investor decisions about your company. For climate, ask: could this physical risk or transition risk affect our revenues, costs, assets, or access to capital in a way that investors would want to know about?

For a coastal hotel chain: sea level rise and storm surge are financially material. For a software company with no manufacturing: likely not material to physical risk, but a data center in a water-stressed region might be.

### Impact Materiality (Required Under CSRD)

Under CSRD double materiality, you also need to assess impact materiality: do your operations or supply chain have a significant impact on the climate? A steel manufacturer with high direct emissions has high impact materiality for climate. A financial services firm that funds fossil fuel projects has high financed emissions.

### How to Do a Materiality Assessment

A practical approach for a small or medium business:

1. List the climate topics that could be relevant (physical risk categories, transition risk categories, your own emissions and targets)
2. For each, score: (a) likelihood of occurrence or change, (b) potential magnitude of financial or business impact
3. For impact materiality under CSRD: score your actual or potential impact on the climate for each topic
4. Prioritize the topics that score high on both likelihood and magnitude
5. Focus your disclosure on those high-priority topics

## Scope 1, 2, and 3 Emissions: What to Measure and How

### The Three Scopes Explained

**Scope 1 (Direct emissions)**: Emissions from sources you own or control.
- Burning natural gas in a furnace you own
- Fuel burned in company vehicles
- Fugitive emissions from refrigerants in your cooling systems
- Industrial process emissions (if you manufacture something that releases gases)

**Scope 2 (Purchased energy)**: Emissions from electricity, steam, heat, or cooling you buy from others.
- Electricity from the grid
- District heat or cooling

**Scope 3 (Everything else)**: Emissions in your upstream supply chain, from employees commuting, from customer use of your products, and from disposal of your products.
- Scope 3 Category 1: Purchased goods and services (the manufacturing emissions embedded in what you buy)
- Scope 3 Category 3: Fuel and energy-related activities (upstream emissions from producing the energy you use)
- Scope 3 Category 6: Business travel
- Scope 3 Category 7: Employee commuting
- Scope 3 Category 11: Use of sold products (if your customers burn or use your product to emit greenhouse gases)
- Scope 3 Category 15: Investments (for financial firms: the emissions of companies they invest in or lend to)

### What Is Required by Each Framework

**TCFD**: Scope 1 and 2 are expected. Scope 3 is recommended if material.

**SEC rules (US listed companies)**: Scope 1 and 2 required. Scope 3 required only if material or if included in your targets.

**CSRD/ESRS E1**: All three scopes required for large in-scope companies. Scope 3 has more flexibility for phased implementation.

**ISSB S2**: Scope 1 and 2 required. Scope 3 required if material.

### How to Measure Emissions at Small Scale

**Scope 1**: Multiply fuel consumption (gallons, therms, liters) by published emission factors. EPA provides these free at epa.gov/climateleadership/ghg-inventory-guidance.

**Scope 2**: Your electricity bill in kWh multiplied by your grid emission factor. The EPA eGRID database gives US grid factors by region. Your utility may provide one.

**Scope 3, abbreviated approach**: For most small businesses, start with the two or three most material Scope 3 categories. Business travel is straightforward (use ICAO flight calculator for flights, EPA factors for car miles). Purchased goods is harder; GHG Protocol provides spend-based emission factors (using industry-average emission intensities applied to spending by category) which are a reasonable approximation.

**Free tools**: World Resources Institute GHG Protocol calculation tools at ghgprotocol.org/calculation-tools. SME Climate Hub at smeclimatehub.org has a simplified calculator for companies under 250 employees.

## ISSB S1 and S2: The New Global Baseline

The International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB) issued its first two standards in 2023:

**ISSB S1 (General Requirements for Disclosure of Sustainability-related Financial Information)**: The overarching framework for all sustainability-related financial disclosures. Requires disclosure of material sustainability-related risks and opportunities across governance, strategy, risk management, and metrics and targets.

**ISSB S2 (Climate-related Disclosures)**: The climate-specific standard. Builds on and effectively supersedes TCFD. Requires all four TCFD pillars plus industry-specific metrics (drawn from SASB standards for each industry).

**Jurisdiction adoption**: Many jurisdictions are adopting ISSB standards as their mandatory baseline, including the UK (planned for large companies), Australia (mandatory for large listed companies phasing in from 2025), Canada, Singapore, and others. The EU's CSRD uses its own ESRS standards but with an equivalence process with ISSB underway.

**The key difference from TCFD**: ISSB requires quantitative, industry-specific metrics (not just qualitative disclosure) and requires Scope 3 emissions if material. It is more prescriptive and harder to satisfy with general narrative.

## What Your Bank Is Now Asking

Financial institutions that have signed the Net-Zero Banking Alliance (NZBA) or adopted the Equator Principles are increasingly incorporating climate risk into lending decisions. Here is what the questionnaires typically cover:

- **Greenhouse gas inventory**: Do you have one? Scope 1 and 2 at minimum.
- **Physical risk screening**: Have you assessed which of your facilities or operations face physical climate risks?
- **Transition risk**: Are you in a sector subject to carbon pricing, technology disruption, or regulatory change?
- **Targets**: Have you committed to emissions reduction targets? Are they time-bound with interim milestones?
- **Governance**: Is climate risk discussed at board level?

For commercial property loans, lenders are increasingly incorporating the energy efficiency and flood risk of the collateral property into lending decisions. A building in a high flood risk zone or with a poor energy performance certificate (in the UK) is now being priced differently.

## Common Gaps That Get Flagged

Based on common review findings across TCFD reports and sustainability disclosures:

**No scenario analysis, or scenarios that are too vague**: Saying "we considered two scenarios" without describing what they are and what the results showed does not satisfy the requirement. Disclosure needs to be specific about scenario parameters and financial impacts.

**Missing Scope 3**: Companies that set "net zero" targets that only cover Scope 1 and 2 are increasingly flagged by investors and standards bodies. For most companies, Scope 3 is the majority of their total emissions.

**Targets without interim milestones**: A 2050 net zero target with no interim milestones (2030 targets, five-year milestones) lacks credibility. Frameworks are increasingly requiring specific near-term milestones.

**Governance on paper but no accountability**: Stating that the board oversees sustainability without showing how that oversight works in practice (what information the board receives, what decisions it takes) does not satisfy governance disclosure requirements.

**No description of how climate risk connects to financial planning**: Good disclosure connects the risk assessment to actual financial impact (capex, operating costs, revenue risk). Narrative-only disclosure that does not quantify even order-of-magnitude impacts is increasingly viewed as insufficient.

**Boilerplate language**: Regurgitating framework definitions without applying them to the specific business. Reviewers want to understand the specific risks for your business, not a generic summary of what climate change means in general.

## Disclosure Timeline

### What Is Required Now (as of 2025)

- **EU companies meeting Wave 1 CSRD thresholds**: Full ESRS E1 disclosure for fiscal year 2024 reports
- **UK large companies and listed premium companies**: TCFD-aligned disclosure in Annual Reports
- **US listed large accelerated filers**: SEC rules implementation (subject to legal status)
- **Financial institutions signed to NZBA or TCFD**: Annual TCFD disclosure

### Coming in 1 to 2 Years

- **EU Wave 2 companies (250+ employees or EUR 40M turnover or EUR 20M assets)**: CSRD reporting required for fiscal year 2025
- **Australia large listed companies**: ISSB-aligned reporting phasing in

### Coming in 3 to 5 Years

- **EU Wave 3 (listed SMEs)**: CSRD reporting from fiscal year 2026
- **EU Wave 4 (non-EU companies with large EU operations)**: From 2028
- **Broader ISSB adoption globally**: As more jurisdictions adopt ISSB S2 as their mandatory standard

## Quick Reference Checklist

### Understanding your obligations
- [ ] Identify whether you meet CSRD Wave 1 or Wave 2 thresholds (employees, turnover, total assets)
- [ ] If US listed: check current status of SEC climate rules and your filing category
- [ ] If UK large company: confirm TCFD alignment requirement applies to you
- [ ] Identify whether large customers or lenders are asking for climate data (Scope 3 pull-through)

### Building your disclosure foundation
- [ ] Complete a greenhouse gas inventory covering at least Scope 1 and 2 using GHG Protocol methodology
- [ ] Identify your most material physical risks using free tools (WRI Aqueduct for water, NOAA/First Street for flood)
- [ ] Identify your most material transition risks (carbon pricing, technology disruption, regulatory change in your sector)
- [ ] Document governance: which committee or executive owns climate risk, how often is it reviewed at board level

### Scenario analysis (simplified)
- [ ] Select two scenarios: a 1.5 degree C orderly transition and a 3+ degree C insufficient action scenario
- [ ] For each scenario, identify your top three business risks and order-of-magnitude financial impacts
- [ ] Document your strategic response to each scenario

### Metrics and targets
- [ ] Complete a Scope 3 screening to identify your most material Scope 3 categories
- [ ] Set a greenhouse gas reduction target with a base year, target year, and interim milestones
- [ ] If committing to net zero: ensure your definition covers Scope 3 and aligns with Science Based Targets initiative (SBTi) methodology

### Common gap checks
- [ ] Scenario analysis includes specific parameters and financial impact estimates, not just narrative
- [ ] Targets include interim milestones, not just a 2050 endpoint
- [ ] Governance disclosure describes the actual process, not just who is responsible
- [ ] Physical and transition risks are connected to financial planning (capex, operating cost, revenue scenarios)
