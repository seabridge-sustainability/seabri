# Wildfire Risk Assessment

A practical guide for homeowners, farmers, and rural businesses in fire-prone areas — how to find your risk, reduce it, protect your insurance, and make sound financial decisions about properties where wildfire is a real threat.

---

## The Wildland-Urban Interface: What It Is and Why It Matters

The **Wildland-Urban Interface (WUI)** is where homes and other structures are built near or within wildland vegetation — forests, shrublands, grasslands. This is where fire burns structures.

**Scale**: Approximately 1 in 3 US homes — roughly 44 million properties — is located in or near the WUI. This is not a California-specific problem. The WUI extends across the South, Mountain West, Pacific Northwest, upper Midwest, and into the Great Plains as drought-stressed grasslands expand.

**Urban fire risk is different**: WUI fire and urban fire are fundamentally different threats. In WUI fires, the primary driver is vegetation — fuel load, moisture, wind. In dense urban areas, structure-to-structure fire spread dominates (as in the 2023 Lahaina, Hawaii fire). A building that is hardened against ember ignition behaves very differently than one that sits next to a wooden fence line running toward a neighbor's garage. Know which scenario you are in.

## How Wildfire Risk Is Mapped

### CAL FIRE Fire Hazard Severity Zones (FHSZ) — California

**What it is**: Regulatory maps covering all of California. Three severity categories: Moderate, High, and Very High. Based on fire behavior modeling — fuel load, slope, wind exposure, ember production potential. Updated most recently in 2021-2023.

**Where**: osfm.fire.ca.gov/wildfire-risk-reduction-and-recovery/fire-hazard-severity-zones/

**Why it matters beyond knowledge**: Being in a Very High FHSZ triggers specific California building code requirements for new construction and substantial remodeling: Class A fire-rated roofing, ember-resistant vents, specific deck materials, and defensible space compliance. It also affects insurance eligibility and pricing directly.

**Two zones**: State Responsibility Area (SRA) — CAL FIRE jurisdiction, rural and unincorporated areas. Local Responsibility Area (LRA) — within incorporated cities and some counties. Some LRA zones exist inside city limits, particularly for hillside neighborhoods.

### USFS National Forest and Rangeland Hazard Zones

**What it is**: USFS hazard mapping for lands adjacent to National Forests and Grasslands. Less regulatory than CAL FIRE zones but important for rural properties and farms near federal land.

**Where**: fs.usda.gov — search "wildland fire" or contact your local ranger district.

**Note**: The USFS fire suppression capacity is a factor in how quickly fires near federal land get resources. Properties adjacent to National Forest land often have longer response times due to jurisdiction complexity.

### First Street Foundation Wildfire Risk Factor — Free

**What it is**: Individual property wildfire risk scores (1-10 scale) accounting for current and projected future conditions out to 30 years. Updated more frequently than regulatory maps and uses current climate data.

**Where**: riskfactor.com — free property-level lookup.

**Score interpretation**:
- 1-4: Low to minimal risk
- 5-7: Moderate — review mitigation options
- 8-10: High to severe — defensible space and home hardening are critical

**Why this matters alongside regulatory maps**: A property may be in a "Moderate" FHSZ zone but score 8 on First Street if fuel conditions, local wind patterns, or recent fire history have elevated risk beyond what the regulatory map captures. Use both.

### National Interagency Fire Center (NIFC)

**Where**: nifc.gov — free access to historical fire perimeters, current fire information, national fire statistics.

**Best used for**: Understanding whether major fires have burned near your property in the past, how fire season length has changed in your region.

## The Ember Reality: What Actually Burns Homes

Research from the Insurance Institute for Business and Home Safety (IBHS), CAL FIRE, and the US Forest Service consistently finds: **approximately 90% of structures that burn in wildfire are ignited by embers (firebrands), not by direct contact with the fire front.**

Embers are burning fragments of wood, bark, or debris lofted by the fire's convection column. They can travel **1 to 2 miles ahead of the fire front**, land on roofs, in gutters, against siding, in vents, and on decks — and smolder until they ignite.

**The implication**: The critical question for a homeowner is not "can the fire reach my property?" but "can embers land on or near my home and find combustible material?"

This shifts mitigation priority from distant landscape management toward the **ember resistance of the structure itself** and the immediate zone around it.

## Defensible Space: Three Zones, Specific Rules

Defensible space is the managed buffer around your home that slows approaching fire, reduces ember landing zones, and gives firefighters a workable area. California law requires 100 feet of defensible space for properties in State Responsibility Areas. Evidence consistently shows it works.

### Zone 0 — The Non-Combustible Zone (0-5 feet from the structure)

Zone 0 is a relatively new addition to defensible space guidance, emerging from post-fire research on how homes actually ignite. This zone should contain **no combustible material whatsoever**.

**What this means specifically**:
- No wood mulch — use gravel, decomposed granite, pebbles, or bare soil
- No decorative bark chips against the foundation
- No wood decking or wood planter boxes attached to the structure
- No propane tanks, woodpiles, outdoor furniture, doormats, or anything that burns within 5 feet
- Ember-resistant vent screens on all attic, soffit, and foundation vents
- No gaps where embers can enter — check where pipes, cables, and wiring enter the structure and seal with non-combustible material
- Clean gutters and roof valleys of all debris before fire season

Zone 0 is where professional fire engineers say the highest-impact per-dollar changes can be made. It costs relatively little and dramatically reduces the probability your home ignites from embers.

### Zone 1 — Lean, Clean, and Green (5-30 feet from the structure)

The goal in Zone 1 is to eliminate any vegetation or material that could carry fire to your home or that landing embers could ignite.

**What to do**:
- Remove dead plants, dry leaves, pine needles, and accumulated debris
- Mow lawns and grasses to under 4 inches; well-irrigated lawns are significantly less flammable
- Trim tree branches to at least 10 feet above the ground
- Remove shrubs and low vegetation under tree canopies (eliminates "ladder fuels")
- Space plants in groups with non-combustible gaps between them
- Remove woodpiles, compost bins, and propane tanks — relocate outside Zone 1 or to an enclosed non-combustible structure
- Use non-combustible mulch (gravel, decomposed granite) throughout this zone

### Zone 2 — Reduced Fuel Load (30-100 feet from the structure, or to property line)

Zone 2 reduces the overall intensity of fire approaching your property.

**What to do**:
- Space trees so there are 10-foot gaps between canopies (wider on slopes steeper than 20%)
- Remove shrubs and brush in clusters, creating irregular fuel breaks
- Eliminate ladder fuels — lower branches and shrubs allowing fire to climb from ground to tree canopies
- Remove dead vegetation throughout
- Mow grass to under 4 inches during dry season

**Slope effect**: Fire moves dramatically faster uphill. On a 30% slope, fire can travel up to 4x faster than on flat ground. If your home is above vegetation on a slope, extend Zone 2 requirements downhill further than 100 feet.

## Home Hardening: What the Research Shows Matters Most

### Roof — Highest Priority

The roof is the most vulnerable surface in a wildfire. Embers land on roofs and ignite accumulated debris or directly ignite the roof material.

- **Class A fire-rated roofing**: Metal (aluminum, steel, copper), concrete tile, clay tile, Class A-rated asphalt shingles all qualify. These are the highest fire-resistance rating.
- **Wood shake roofing**: Extremely high risk. Uninsurable or extremely expensive in most high-risk zones. If you have wood shake, replacing it is the single highest-impact hardening action available.
- **Gutter maintenance**: Gutters fill with leaf and needle debris — dry debris is an ember catcher. An ember landing in a full gutter can ignite debris, which ignites the roof fascia. Install gutter guards or clean gutters at the start of fire season every year.

### Vents — High Impact per Dollar

Attic vents, soffit vents, and foundation vents with standard 1/4-inch mesh allow embers through. Embers enter the attic, find dry insulation or wood framing, and ignite from inside — the home burns from within.

- **Replace with ember-resistant vent products**: 1/16-inch or smaller metal mesh, or products specifically rated for ember resistance (meeting ASTM E2886 standard). Brands include Brandguard, O'Hagin, and others.
- **Cost**: Approximately $15-40 per vent. For a typical home with 10-15 vents, this is $150-600 — one of the highest-impact investments per dollar available.

### Deck and Siding

Decks ignite from embers landing and smoldering in debris accumulated in cracks and gaps.

- **Composite or non-combustible decking**: Trex and similar composite products are less flammable than wood. Fiber cement, concrete, and aluminum are better options.
- **Under the deck**: A primary ignition zone. Woodpiles, leaf litter, and stored materials under decks are frequent ignition points. Clear entirely or enclose with non-combustible lattice.
- **Siding**: Fiber cement (James Hardie) is significantly more fire-resistant than wood. Stucco is good. Vinyl melts and falls away, exposing structure.

### Windows

Single-pane windows break from radiant heat before flame contact. Once broken, embers and flames enter directly.

- **Double-pane or triple-pane** windows are significantly more resistant to radiant heat.
- **Tempered glass** offers better thermal resistance than standard glass.
- For windows facing dense vegetation or neighboring structures: most critical to upgrade.

## Insurance: A Rapidly Changing Landscape

### What Is Happening With Carrier Non-Renewals

As of 2024-2025, multiple major carriers have stopped writing new homeowner policies or have non-renewed existing policies in high-risk areas. California has seen exits from State Farm, Allstate, and others. This is spreading to portions of Colorado, Oregon, Idaho, and other western states.

This is not a temporary disruption. Insurers are responding to modeled risk that has exceeded what they can price profitably — losses in California alone in 2017-2021 exceeded $30 billion. When carriers withdraw from a market, they typically do not return quickly.

### California FAIR Plan

The California FAIR Plan is the state's insurer of last resort for properties that cannot obtain standard coverage. Coverage limits have been increased (as of 2024, to $3 million for residential dwellings), but:
- Premiums are significantly higher than standard market rates
- Coverage is more limited — basic fire coverage, not a full homeowner's policy
- You will need a "difference in conditions" (DIC) policy alongside it for broader coverage

Every state has an equivalent — search "[your state] FAIR Plan" or "[your state] insurer of last resort."

### How to Protect Your Insurability

1. **Document mitigation**: Before applying for any policy or at renewal, photograph and document your Zone 0, Zone 1, and Zone 2 clearance and all hardening measures. Some carriers require physical inspection; documentation helps in all cases.
2. **Ask specifically about mitigation discounts**: CAL FIRE's Safer From Wildfires program defines specific mitigation tiers; some carriers offer discounts aligned to these tiers. Ask your agent explicitly.
3. **Shop before you receive a non-renewal**: Non-renewals give you 60-75 days. Start shopping before the notice arrives if your property is in a high-risk zone.
4. **Work with an independent agent**: Captive agents (State Farm, Allstate) can only offer one carrier. Independent agents can access dozens.

### Financial Implications of High-Risk Designation

**Property values**: Academic research and CoreLogic analysis are finding measurable "climate risk discounts" in property values in high-risk fire zones — estimated at 3-10% in some California markets, with the discount growing as insurance costs rise.

**Insurance cost trends**: Annual premiums in Very High FHSZ zones that were $1,500-2,500 five years ago are now commonly $4,000-10,000+. FAIR Plan rates in some areas exceed $15,000/year for standard coverage.

**Financing**: If a property becomes uninsurable through standard carriers and requires FAIR Plan, some lenders will not originate or renew mortgages, or will do so only at less favorable terms. This limits the buyer pool and compresses valuations.

**The buying decision**: If you are purchasing in a high-risk zone, obtain insurance quotes before making an offer — not after. Insurance availability and cost are now material to the transaction and should be treated as such.

## Climate Change and Fire: Specific Mechanisms

**Longer fire seasons**: The average US fire season has extended by 78 days since the 1970s. In California, fire now occurs year-round.

**Vapor pressure deficit (VPD)**: VPD measures how aggressively the atmosphere pulls moisture from vegetation. High VPD desiccates live vegetation faster, effectively converting living plants to fine fuels. VPD is increasing across the West with warming temperatures.

**Fuel accumulation and drought-stressed vegetation**: Trees stressed by drought and bark beetles die standing and become highly flammable. Millions of acres of dead standing timber have accumulated in the Sierra Nevada, Cascades, and Rocky Mountains.

**Changing precipitation patterns**: Some regions are seeing precipitation compressed into fewer, more intense events, with longer dry periods between. More precipitation in winter followed by a drier fire season increases grass growth (fuel) and subsequent fire risk.

**Geographic expansion**: Fire risk is moving into the South, the Great Plains, and parts of the East Coast. Your local fire history from 20 years ago is not a reliable guide to current risk.

## Evacuation: Real Preparation

**The consistent finding from fire safety research and fire service professionals: evacuate early.** The decision to shelter in place is rarely wise and requires a highly hardened structure and an active, prepared occupant.

**Evacuate at the first warning, not the order**: A warning is the earlier alert. An order means fire is imminent. Late evacuation into smoke-reduced visibility and gridlock is dangerous.

**Practical preparation**:
- Know at least two evacuation routes from your property — routes can be cut off by fire
- Know the location of your local emergency shelter or evacuation destination in advance
- Pre-position a "go bag": medications, documents (insurance policy, deed, IDs), a change of clothes, cash, phone chargers, water
- Scan and cloud-store key documents — if your home burns, paper documents burn with it
- Know how to manually open your garage door (electric openers require power)
- Plan for pets and livestock — this is frequently what causes people to delay or return

**Livestock and farm animals**: For farmers and rural operations, evacuation planning for animals is critical and takes more time. Identify trailer capacity, evacuation destination, and route in advance. Some counties have livestock evacuation coordination through the county agricultural commissioner.

## Quick Reference Checklist

**Data and risk awareness**:
- [ ] Look up property at riskfactor.com — note wildfire score and 30-year projection
- [ ] Check CAL FIRE FHSZ designation (California) at osfm.fire.ca.gov
- [ ] Review local fire history and perimeters at nifc.gov
- [ ] Know your local evacuation routes (minimum two)

**Zone 0 (0-5 ft)**:
- [ ] All combustible material removed — no wood mulch, no woodpiles, no propane tanks
- [ ] Gravel or decomposed granite used as ground cover
- [ ] All vents replaced with ember-resistant products (1/16-inch or finer mesh)
- [ ] Gutters clear of debris; gutter guards considered

**Zone 1 (5-30 ft)**:
- [ ] Dead vegetation removed; lawn mowed to under 4 inches
- [ ] Tree branches trimmed to 10 ft clearance
- [ ] Ladder fuels removed under tree canopies

**Zone 2 (30-100 ft)**:
- [ ] Trees spaced with 10-ft canopy gaps (wider on slopes)
- [ ] Brush cleared, ladder fuels removed, grass mowed

**Home hardening**:
- [ ] Roof: Class A-rated material? If wood shake — replace this first
- [ ] Vents: ember-resistant products installed
- [ ] Deck: debris cleared; under-deck storage removed
- [ ] Siding: fiber cement or stucco preferred
- [ ] Windows: double-pane minimum facing vegetation

**Insurance**:
- [ ] Document all defensible space and hardening with photos
- [ ] Review policy for coverage limits and exclusions
- [ ] Ask agent specifically about mitigation discounts
- [ ] If non-renewed: shop private market immediately, then check state FAIR Plan

**Evacuation**:
- [ ] Two evacuation routes identified and driven
- [ ] Go bag assembled and accessible
- [ ] Key documents scanned and cloud-stored
- [ ] Livestock/pet evacuation plan if applicable
