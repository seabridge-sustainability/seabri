# Home Resilience Audit

A practical guide for homeowners who want to assess their home's exposure to climate risks, prioritize improvements intelligently, and take advantage of tax credits and financing to pay for them.

---

## Start With Your Risk Profile: Multiple Hazards, One Home

Before spending money on improvements, understand which hazards actually threaten your property. Most homes face more than one, and the priority order of improvements differs substantially depending on what you face.

**Flood**: Look up your address at riskfactor.com (First Street Foundation) for a current risk score and 30-year projection. Also check msc.fema.gov for your official FEMA flood zone. If coastal, check NOAA's Sea Level Rise Viewer at coast.noaa.gov/slr.

**Wildfire**: Check riskfactor.com for your wildfire risk score (1-10). If in California, check your Fire Hazard Severity Zone at osfm.fire.ca.gov. Rural properties near wildland vegetation should also check nifc.gov for local fire history.

**Extreme heat**: Use the CDC's Heat and Health Tracker at ephtracking.cdc.gov or NOAA's heat risk maps at nws.noaa.gov. Urban properties face an additional urban heat island effect — surface temperatures 5-10°F higher than surrounding rural areas due to pavement and rooftops.

**Wind and storm**: FEMA's coastal flood maps identify hurricane wind zones. NOAA storm reports at ncdc.noaa.gov show your area's history of severe wind events, hail, and tornadoes.

**Once you know your risk profile**: A property in coastal Louisiana faces very different priorities than one in the East Bay hills or the Phoenix metro. Do not buy a generator before understanding whether your primary risk is flooding rather than power outage. Do not add solar panels before addressing the audit failures that are costing you money today.

## Energy Audit Basics: What You Don't Know Is Costing You

### The Blower Door Test

A blower door test uses a calibrated fan mounted in a door frame to depressurize your home. The air needed to maintain that pressure drop measures total air leakage — stated in cubic feet per minute at 50 Pascals of pressure (CFM50) or as Air Changes per Hour (ACH50).

**What the numbers mean**:
- ACH50 under 1.0: Very tight — typical of high-performance new construction
- ACH50 1.0-5.0: Moderately tight — acceptable range for retrofitted existing homes
- ACH50 5.0-10.0: Leaky — significant air sealing opportunity
- ACH50 over 10.0: Very leaky — common in pre-1980 homes; major opportunity

**Who conducts this**: A certified energy auditor. The Building Performance Institute (BPI) and RESNET certify auditors nationally. Find one at bpi.org or natresnet.org. Cost: typically $300-600. Many utilities offer subsidized audits — call your utility before booking privately.

**Why this matters for resilience specifically**: A leaky home is harder to keep cool during extreme heat events and loses the ability to passively maintain temperature after a power outage. A tight, well-insulated home stays livable several hours longer during an outage — potentially critical in a heat wave.

### Infrared Thermography

Thermal imaging cameras show temperature differences across surfaces. Cold spots on walls and ceilings (during heating season) reveal insulation gaps or air leakage pathways that visual inspection cannot find.

**What it finds**: Missing insulation batts near attic eaves, insulation that has settled or compressed, thermal bridging through framing members, air leakage around penetrations.

**Usually included in professional energy audits**. Consumer-grade thermal cameras (Flir makes models for under $300) allow DIY assessment.

### Where Heat Escapes: The Priority Order

Research from Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory and the US Department of Energy finds these as the largest sources of energy loss in US homes:

1. **Air leakage** (gaps, cracks, penetrations): 25-40% of heating and cooling load in leaky homes. The single most cost-effective category to address.
2. **Attic insulation**: Heat rises. An under-insulated attic is a major thermal bypass. Target R-38 to R-60 depending on climate zone.
3. **Wall insulation**: Older homes often have no wall insulation. Injection foam or blown-in insulation can be added without removing drywall in many cases.
4. **Windows and doors**: Important for comfort but often overstated as an energy investment. Long payback periods unless you are starting from single-pane.
5. **Foundation and crawl space**: Significant in homes with uninsulated crawl spaces or uninsulated slab edges.

## The Investment Hierarchy: What to Do in What Order

**The sequence matters more than most people realize.** A heat pump sized for a leaky home will be oversized after you air-seal and insulate — running inefficiently. Solar panels on an inefficient envelope generate power to compensate for waste rather than displacing grid energy. Getting the order right saves money at every step.

**Step 1: Air sealing** — cheapest, highest return on investment. Seal penetrations around pipes, wires, HVAC ducts, recessed lights, and attic hatches. A professional air sealing job runs $500-2,500 depending on home size. Motivated homeowners can do accessible areas themselves with caulk, foam, and weatherstripping.

**Step 2: Insulation** — after air sealing, insulation works much better because you have eliminated the pathways that bypass it. Attic first, then crawl space or basement if applicable.

**Step 3: Heating and cooling equipment** — after the envelope is tightened, upgrade to a heat pump sized to the actual reduced load of your improved home.

**Step 4: Water heating** — heat pump water heaters use 60-70% less electricity than resistance water heaters. Switch before adding solar so your solar system is sized to your reduced demand.

**Step 5: Solar** — after efficiency improvements, add solar sized to your actual remaining consumption. You will need a smaller system — saving money on the solar itself.

**Step 6: Battery storage** — if grid reliability is a concern (wildfire evacuation areas, frequent outages, extreme heat grid stress), add battery storage sized to power your critical loads.

## Inflation Reduction Act Tax Credits: Specific Numbers

The Inflation Reduction Act (IRA), signed August 2022, extended and expanded residential clean energy tax credits through 2032. These are dollar-for-dollar reductions in your federal income tax owed — not deductions.

### Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit (Section 25C)

Applies to improvements on existing primary residences. The annual cap is **$3,200 per year** with category sub-limits. You can claim this credit every year through 2032 — not just once — which allows you to plan improvements across multiple tax years.

**Heat pumps (space heating/cooling)**: Up to **$2,000** credit per year. This is a higher separate cap from the general $1,200 limit.

**Heat pump water heater**: Up to **$2,000** credit (shares the heat pump category cap with space heating).

**Insulation and air sealing materials**: Up to **$1,200** per year combined.

**Home energy audit**: Up to **$150** credit for a qualified home energy audit.

**Energy Star windows**: Up to **$600** per year.

**Exterior doors**: Up to **$500** per year for qualifying doors.

**Electric panel upgrade**: Up to **$600** per year (when required to support a new heat pump or EV charger).

**Income limits**: The 25C credit has no income limit. Any homeowner with federal tax liability can claim it.

**How to claim**: IRS Form 5695 filed with your annual return. Keep receipts and manufacturer's certification statements.

### Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D)

**Solar panels (photovoltaic)**: **30% tax credit** on the total installed cost. No annual dollar cap. A $22,000 solar system generates a $6,600 federal credit.

**Battery storage (standalone)**: **30% credit** for battery systems of 3 kWh capacity or greater — as of 2023, this applies to standalone batteries, not just batteries paired with solar. A $12,000 battery system generates a $3,600 credit.

**Geothermal heat pumps**: 30% credit through 2032.

**Solar water heating**: 30% credit if at least half of the home's water heating comes from solar.

**Income limits**: The 25D credit has no income limit.

**Timeline**: 30% through 2032, stepping to 26% in 2033, 22% in 2034, then expiring unless renewed.

### EV Tax Credit (Clean Vehicle Credit, Section 30D)

**New EVs**: Up to **$7,500** for qualifying new vehicles. Income limits: $150,000 for single filers, $300,000 for married filing jointly. Vehicle MSRP limits: $55,000 for passenger cars, $80,000 for trucks, vans, and SUVs.

**Used EVs**: Up to **$4,000** or 30% of sale price (whichever is less) for qualifying used EVs purchased from a dealer. Income limits: $75,000 single, $150,000 joint.

**Point-of-sale transfer**: Since January 2024, dealers can transfer the credit to buyers at point of sale — reducing the purchase price directly rather than requiring you to wait for your tax refund.

## State and Utility Rebates: Stacking Incentives

Federal credits and state or utility rebates can be combined — this is called "stacking." It is legal and encouraged.

**IRA High-Efficiency Electric Home Rebate Act (HEEHRA)**: A separate IRA program (distinct from the tax credits) providing point-of-sale rebates through states for households below 150% of area median income. Up to $14,000 total per household for qualifying upgrades: heat pump HVAC (up to $8,000), heat pump water heater ($1,750), electric stove ($840), electrical panel upgrades ($4,000), weatherization ($1,600). Program rollout varies by state — check your state energy office.

**State programs**: Many states have independent rebate programs. California's Energy Upgrade California, New York's NY-Sun and EmPower+ programs, Massachusetts Clean Energy Center, and others offer meaningful additional incentives. Find your state program at dsireusa.org (Database of State Incentives for Renewables and Efficiency).

**Utility rebates**: Most large utilities offer rebates for qualifying equipment. Call your utility or check their website. Common: $100-1,500 for heat pump water heaters, $25-150 for smart thermostats, $500-2,000 for heat pump HVAC.

**How stacking works in practice**: A heat pump installation costing $9,000 might yield a $2,000 IRA 25C tax credit + $800 utility rebate + $1,200 state rebate = $4,000 in combined incentives, bringing your net cost to $5,000. Then applying for the HEEHRA point-of-sale rebate (if income-eligible) could reduce it further. Run the numbers with all three before signing a contract.

**Important note**: Some rebates must be applied before you claim the federal credit; others apply to the post-rebate cost. Read the specific program rules for each rebate to understand how they interact.

## PACE Financing

Property Assessed Clean Energy (PACE) financing allows homeowners to finance qualifying improvements through a special assessment added to the property tax bill — no upfront cost and no traditional loan qualification.

**How it works**: A PACE lender pays your contractor directly. You repay the assessment through your property tax bill over 5-25 years. If you sell the property, the assessment transfers to the buyer (or is paid off at closing).

**What qualifies**: Energy efficiency improvements, renewable energy, water efficiency, and in some states, wildfire hardening and other resilience measures.

**Pros**: No upfront cash required; repayment is with property taxes (potentially tax-deductible as property tax); does not require traditional credit score approval; can finance projects that are hard to fund with conventional loans; can fund resilience improvements (like roofing) that are not eligible for other incentives.

**Cons**: Interest rates are typically higher than home equity loans (often 6-10%); the assessment transfers with the property — some buyers are unwilling to assume it, which can complicate sale; some mortgage lenders do not permit PACE assessments on properties without their consent; PACE has faced consumer protection concerns, particularly regarding predatory origination practices in some states.

**State availability**: PACE is currently available in California, Florida, Missouri, and growing. Check at pacenation.org for current state-by-state availability.

**When PACE makes sense**: If you have significant improvements to make, limited cash or home equity, and want to move forward without waiting, PACE is worth evaluating — particularly if your state's program has good consumer protections. Compare total cost over the repayment period against alternatives including home equity line of credit (HELOC) or personal loan.

## Heat Pump Deep Dive

Heat pumps move heat rather than generate it. In heating mode, they extract heat from outside air (even in cold conditions) and deliver it indoors. In cooling mode, they reverse the cycle. This physics makes them 2-4x more efficient than electric resistance heating.

**Mini-split (ductless) heat pumps**: Individual air handlers installed in rooms or zones, connected to an outdoor compressor. No ductwork required — ideal for homes without existing ducts, room additions, or targeted zone control. Highly efficient. Cost: $3,000-10,000 per zone installed, depending on capacity and contractor.

**Ducted central heat pump**: Replaces a gas furnace and central air conditioning system using existing ductwork. If you have a forced-air system, a ducted heat pump replaces both units. Cost: $5,000-15,000 installed depending on home size and system complexity.

**Heat pump water heater**: Extracts heat from surrounding air to heat water rather than using resistance heating elements. Uses 60-70% less electricity than a conventional electric water heater — typically $300-600 in annual savings. Requires a minimum surrounding air volume (at least 700-1,000 cubic feet — a standard mechanical room or basement works). Does not perform well in very cold, uninsulated spaces because it draws heat from the surrounding air. Cost: $1,200-2,200 installed. IRA credit up to $2,000.

**Cold climate performance**: Modern cold-climate heat pumps operate effectively down to -15°F or colder. This is a significant advance over older equipment and eliminates the concern about performance in northern US winters. Look for units with the Heating Seasonal Performance Factor (HSPF2) rating of 9.0 or higher for good efficiency.

**When switching from gas makes financial sense**: Use the Rewiring America calculator at rewiringamerica.org/savings for a home-specific estimate. As a general rule: with electricity rates below $0.20/kWh, heat pumps typically out-compete gas economically even before counting the IRA credit. Above $0.25/kWh (common in California, New England, Hawaii), the economics depend more on local gas prices. After the $2,000 IRA credit is applied, the threshold shifts further in favor of heat pumps in most markets.

**The right time to switch**: When your furnace or air conditioner is near end of life (typically 15-20 years). Replacing a working gas furnace with years of life remaining has a longer payback period than waiting until the natural replacement point.

## Solar and Battery Storage: How to Evaluate a Proposal

### Net Metering Explained

Net metering allows you to export excess solar generation to the grid in exchange for a credit on your bill. The value of that credit varies substantially by state and utility and is the most important variable in your payback period.

**Full retail net metering**: You receive credit at the retail electricity rate you would have paid. Historically standard in California; the NEM 3.0 policy change (April 2023) reduced export credits to approximately $0.05/kWh in many cases, dramatically lengthening payback periods for California solar.

**Avoided cost net metering**: You receive only the utility's wholesale cost — sometimes less than a quarter of retail. Common in southern and southeastern utilities.

**Before evaluating any solar proposal**: Confirm your utility's current net metering policy. Call the utility and ask what rate you receive for exported power. This single piece of information changes everything about whether solar makes financial sense for you right now.

### Questions to Ask Any Solar Installer

- What is the annual production estimate in kWh? How does it compare to my actual annual usage?
- What degradation rate does the production model assume? (Industry standard: 0.5% per year)
- What inverter type is proposed? (Microinverters or DC power optimizers allow panel-level monitoring and perform better with shading; string inverters are cheaper but more sensitive to partial shading from trees or chimneys)
- What is the production warranty on the panels? (25 years at 80%+ of rated output is standard from reputable manufacturers)
- Who is the installing contractor? Are they subcontracting the installation?
- What happens to my warranty and monitoring if the installing company is acquired or goes out of business?

### Payback Period Calculation

Payback period = (Total installed cost — IRA credit — other rebates) / Annual electricity savings

**Example**: $24,000 total installed cost. IRA 25D credit: $7,200 (30%). Net cost: $16,800. Annual electricity savings based on current rate and system production: $1,800/year. Payback: 16,800 / 1,800 = **9.3 years**. System warrantied for 25 years — remaining life after payback: 15.7 years of savings.

**Caveat**: This example assumes electricity rates stay constant. Most analysts use 2-4% annual electricity rate inflation, which shortens the payback period modestly.

### Battery Storage Specifics

Key parameters when comparing battery proposals:

- **Usable capacity (kWh)**: Tesla Powerwall 3 = 13.5 kWh usable; Enphase IQ Battery 10T = 10 kWh usable; Franklin aPower = 13.6 kWh usable.
- **Power output (kW)**: How many appliances it can power simultaneously. Refrigerator + lights + essential outlets: 2-3 kW. Adding a mini-split: 4-5 kW. Whole-home critical loads: 7-10 kW.
- **Round-trip efficiency**: Energy lost charging and discharging. Look for 90%+ efficiency.
- **Warranty**: 10-year warranty with end-of-warranty capacity guarantee (typically 70% of original capacity).
- **IRA credit**: 30% on the full battery system cost for standalone batteries of 3+ kWh.

## Flood Preparedness Measures That Actually Work

For full flood risk assessment, refer to the Flood Risk Screening skill. Key resilience measures for elevated flood risk:

**Elevate HVAC, electrical panel, and water heater**: If floodwater enters, a flooded HVAC system, electrical panel, and water heater are typically total losses — together $15,000-35,000 to replace. Elevating these above the Base Flood Elevation (BFE) is the single highest-impact flood mitigation measure available to a homeowner.

**Backflow prevention valve**: Prevents sewage from pushing backward through floor drains and toilets during flood-related sewer surcharges. Installed by a licensed plumber. Cost: $300-1,500. High impact relative to cost in areas with combined sewer systems.

**Sump pump with battery backup**: Standard sump pumps run on electricity — which fails during storms. Battery backup keeps the sump running when power is out. Essential for any basement in a flood-prone area. Cost: $300-1,000 installed.

## Extreme Heat Preparation

**Attic insulation and air sealing**: The most effective passive cooling improvements. Slows the rate at which outdoor heat conducts into the home — critical when the grid fails during peak heat.

**Exterior window shading**: Awnings, exterior shutters, or shade trees on west and south exposures reduce solar heat gain dramatically without requiring energy. A west-facing window without shading can add substantial cooling load during afternoon hours.

**Window film**: Reflective or low-e window film applied to interior glass can reduce solar heat gain by 40-70%. Cost: $3-8 per square foot, DIY-friendly. Much cheaper than window replacement.

**Ceiling fans**: Do not cool air but reduce perceived temperature by 4-5°F via wind-chill effect. Allow thermostat setpoint to rise 4°F without a change in perceived comfort. 60-70 watts each versus 1,200+ watts for a window AC.

**Pre-cooling**: When a heat wave is forecast, pre-cool your home overnight to a lower temperature than usual. Your home's thermal mass acts as a heat buffer, slowing the rise to uncomfortable temperatures during the event.

**Backup cooling sizing**: A 5,000 BTU window AC requires approximately 500-600 watts. A 12,000 BTU unit: approximately 1,200 watts. A 5,000-watt generator can run two 12,000 BTU units plus other essential loads. Size your backup power to your actual cooling need, not a generic estimate.
