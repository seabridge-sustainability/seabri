# Investment Risk Screening

How to assess climate and nature risks in investments — from physical hazards affecting specific assets to the economic forces reshaping entire industries — and what lenders and institutional investors actually expect to see.

---

## Physical Climate Risk: What It Is and Why It Matters Now

Physical climate risk is the risk that changes in the climate — more frequent extreme weather events and gradual shifts in baseline conditions — damage or destroy assets, disrupt operations, or increase costs. Unlike transition risk, physical risk is already happening. Damages from climate-related disasters in the US exceeded $170 billion in 2023.

Two categories:

**Acute risk**: Discrete events — floods, wildfires, hurricanes, extreme heat waves. These cause immediate asset damage and business interruption.

**Chronic risk**: Gradual, persistent shifts — sea level rise, increasing average temperatures, changing precipitation patterns, water stress. These erode asset values and operating conditions over years to decades.

Both categories matter for any asset with a multi-year hold period.

## Physical Risk Screening by Asset Class

### Real Estate

Location is the primary determinant of physical risk in real estate. The building cannot move.

**What to screen**:
- **Flood zone**: Is the property in FEMA Zone AE (1% annual flood probability), Zone VE (coastal high-velocity), or Zone X? An AE designation means mandatory flood insurance for federally backed mortgages. More importantly, check the **First Street Foundation Risk Factor** at riskfactor.com — many properties in Zone X have First Street scores of 5+ because FEMA maps are often 30+ years old and don't reflect current risk.
- **Wildfire**: CAL FIRE Fire Hazard Severity Zone designation (osfm.fire.ca.gov) for California properties; First Street wildfire score for others. A score of 7+ warrants deeper investigation.
- **Heat**: NOAA Atlas 14 (hdsc.nws.noaa.gov/hdsc/pfds) and Climate Central (climatecentral.org) track heat day trends by location. Properties in Phoenix, Las Vegas, Dallas, and Miami face materially different heat risk today than 20 years ago.
- **Coastal**: NOAA Sea Level Rise Viewer (coast.noaa.gov/slr) shows inundation under 0.5 to 10 feet of sea level rise. Any coastal property within 5 feet of current mean high tide is in a materially different risk environment by 2050.

**Insurance as a leading indicator**: If a property cannot obtain standard homeowner or commercial property insurance through private carriers and must use a state FAIR Plan or insurer of last resort, that is a direct signal that professional risk assessors have concluded the property is too risky to insure at standard rates. This is already affecting financing: some mortgage lenders are tightening standards or declining to originate in uninsurable markets.

**Valuation impacts already visible**: CoreLogic, First Street Foundation, and academic researchers at NBER and the Federal Reserve have documented measurable "climate risk discounts" in property markets:
- Properties with flood history selling at 3-8% discounts in many markets
- Coastal properties in high sea-level-rise-exposure locations showing reduced appreciation relative to inland comparables
- FAIR Plan insurance costs of $8,000-20,000+/year in California high-risk zones materially compressing buyer pool and valuations

### Infrastructure

Roads, bridges, pipelines, power plants, ports, and airports face both direct damage exposure and indirect disruption from supply chain failures. Long-lived assets (30-50+ year design life) are exposed to climate conditions that differ substantially from their design parameters.

**Specific concerns**:
- Power infrastructure in hurricane corridors (Gulf Coast, Carolinas) and wildfire zones (Western US)
- Water infrastructure in water-stressed basins — see WRI Aqueduct (aqueduct.wri.org) for basin-level water risk
- Roads and bridges in permafrost regions (Alaska, Canada) — permafrost thaw is already causing infrastructure failure in arctic and subarctic areas

### Agricultural Land

Drought risk, heat stress on crops and livestock, changing precipitation patterns, and water availability all affect yield and input costs. The geographic distribution of agricultural viability is shifting — some regions gaining, others losing.

**Key tools**:
- **WRI Aqueduct** (aqueduct.wri.org): Free global water stress data by basin. Identifies locations facing baseline water stress, drought risk, and groundwater depletion.
- **USDA Climate Hubs** (climatehubs.usda.gov): Regional climate projections for agriculture by crop type and region.
- **USDA ERS** (ers.usda.gov): Climate impacts on commodity prices and agricultural productivity research.

Irrigation-dependent crops in already water-stressed basins (California Central Valley, Texas High Plains, Colorado River basin) face compounding risk from both climate change and regulatory water allocation changes.

### Supply Chains

Even if a portfolio company's primary facilities are not in a high-risk location, its suppliers may be. Supply chain disruption from physical events is a material risk for manufacturers, retailers, food companies, and pharmaceutical companies with geographically concentrated supplier bases.

**Thailand floods (2011)**: Flooded over 450 factories in a single industrial estate, disrupting global hard drive production for 18 months and causing an estimated $40 billion in economic damage. Companies with single-source Thai suppliers saw revenue impacts lasting years.

**What to screen**: Where are Tier 1 and Tier 2 suppliers concentrated? The CDP Supply Chain program (cdp.net/supply-chain) provides supplier-reported emissions and some climate risk data. For deeper supply chain physical risk, tools like TigerRisk and Supply Wisdom can map geographic exposure.

## Free and Low-Cost Physical Risk Data Sources

**FEMA Flood Map Service Center (msc.fema.gov)**: Official US flood zone designations. Free. Critical limitation: many maps are 30+ years old.

**First Street Foundation Risk Factor (riskfactor.com)**: Property-level flood, wildfire, heat, and wind risk scores for US properties. Incorporates current and 30-year projected risk. Free for individual property lookup. Their **National Risk Index** for communities is also free.

**WRI Aqueduct (aqueduct.wri.org)**: Global water risk by watershed — water stress, drought, riverine and coastal flood risk. Free. Best for agricultural and industrial assets with significant water dependence.

**NOAA Sea Level Rise Viewer (coast.noaa.gov/slr)**: Coastal inundation visualization under different sea level rise scenarios. Free. Essential for any coastal or near-coastal asset.

**USFS Fire Hazard Severity Zones**: CAL FIRE FHSZ maps for California at osfm.fire.ca.gov. Free.

**NOAA Climate Data Online (ncdc.noaa.gov)**: Historical weather data including temperature extremes, hurricane tracks, and precipitation trends. Free.

## Professional Physical Risk Data Providers

**Jupiter Intelligence (jupiterintel.com)**: Asset-level physical climate risk assessment with forward-looking projections. Used by institutional investors and insurance companies. Provides hazard-specific scores (flood, hurricane, heat, wildfire, precipitation) at specific GPS coordinates, with 5-year, 20-year, and 50-year projections under multiple warming scenarios. Pricing available by contract — typically $10,000-100,000+ depending on portfolio size.

**Four Twenty Seven (Moody's) (427mt.com)**: Physical climate risk scores for public equities, real estate, and fixed income. Integrates with Moody's credit analytics. Used by institutional asset managers for portfolio-level physical risk reporting. Acquired by Moody's in 2019.

**MSCI Climate Risk Center (msci.com)**: Physical and transition climate risk data for listed equities and real assets. Their **MSCI Climate Value-at-Risk** model estimates the fraction of asset value exposed to climate change under different scenarios. Required subscription.

**CoreLogic ClimateRisk (corelogic.com)**: Property-level physical risk scoring for US real estate, used by mortgage lenders and insurers. Not typically accessible to individual investors but useful to know lenders are using this data.

**risQ (risq.us)**: Focused on municipal bond physical climate risk. Rates how physical climate hazards affect municipal tax bases and fiscal capacity to repay bonds. Relevant for fixed income investors with municipal exposure.

**When professional data is warranted**: For acquisitions over $20 million involving significant physical assets; for portfolios with concentration in known high-risk geographies; for regulated financial institutions required to conduct climate stress testing.

## Climate Value-at-Risk

Climate value-at-risk (CVaR) is a framework for estimating what fraction of an asset's or portfolio's value is at risk from climate change. It extends traditional value-at-risk thinking to climate scenarios.

**How it works**: Under different warming scenarios (1.5°C, 2°C, 3°C, 4°C), models estimate:
1. **Physical damage costs**: Expected damages to assets from increased frequency and severity of hazards (flooding, heat, storms), discounted to present value
2. **Transition costs**: Expected costs from carbon pricing, regulatory changes, and technology displacement, discounted to present value

MSCI estimates that without mitigation action, physical climate CVaR for global equities could reach 10-20% of portfolio value by 2100 under high-warming scenarios.

**Practical use**: CVaR frameworks let you compare risk across asset classes and geographies on a common basis. A building in Miami coastal real estate and a coal power plant may both have high CVaR, but from different mechanisms — and the mitigation strategies are completely different.

**Limitation**: CVaR models involve significant uncertainty. Use them to identify the direction and order of magnitude of risk, not precise forecasts.

## Transition Risk

Transition risk is the economic disruption caused by the shift away from fossil fuels — through policy, technology, markets, and changing consumer preferences.

### Carbon Price Exposure

Any jurisdiction with a carbon tax or cap-and-trade system increases costs for emissions-intensive operations.

**Active carbon pricing systems as of 2024**:
- **EU Emissions Trading System (ETS)**: ~€50-75/tonne CO2. Covers power, industry, aviation. Being expanded to buildings and road transport by 2027.
- **California Cap-and-Trade**: ~$35-40/tonne. Linked to Quebec.
- **RGGI (Regional Greenhouse Gas Initiative)**: ~$15-20/tonne. Covers power sector in 11 northeastern US states.
- **Canada Carbon Tax**: ~CAD $65/tonne in 2024, rising to CAD $170 by 2030.

**Rough exposure estimate**: Annual carbon price exposure = scope 1 emissions (tonnes CO2e) × carbon price per tonne. A manufacturer with 50,000 tonnes of direct emissions in an EU ETS jurisdiction faces €2.5-3.75 million in direct carbon cost exposure at current prices, rising if prices increase.

World Bank tracks all carbon pricing systems at carbonpricingdashboard.worldbank.org — free.

### Stranded Asset Risk

**Fossil fuel reserves**: Coal, oil, and gas reserves that have been valued on balance sheets may not be economically extractable in a world aligned with 1.5°C-2°C pathways. The International Energy Agency's Net Zero by 2050 scenario requires no new oil and gas field development beyond projects already approved as of 2021. Valuing fossil fuel companies requires assessing what fraction of their reserves can actually be monetized given demand trajectories.

**Fossil fuel infrastructure**: Gas pipelines, LNG terminals, and refineries have 30-40 year asset lives. Investments made today may be worth significantly less if gas demand peaks sooner than companies are pricing in.

**Carbon-intensive real estate**: Buildings that cannot be economically retrofitted to meet tightening energy codes are becoming stranded. Many European cities have or are implementing minimum energy performance standards (MEPS) that require upgrades or restrict leasing of low-rated buildings. The UK has required Energy Performance Certificate (EPC) ratings of E or above for rental properties since 2018; F and G-rated properties cannot legally be rented.

**High-carbon manufacturing**: Steel, cement, aluminum, and chemical facilities with old, inefficient capital stock face increasing cost pressure from carbon pricing and competition from lower-carbon alternatives in development (green hydrogen-based steel, electric arc furnaces, green cement).

### Technology Displacement Risk

**Electric vehicles (EVs)**: ICE vehicle supply chain disruption is not theoretical — it is underway. EV sales share exceeded 25% in Europe and China in 2023. Companies with heavy capital investment in ICE powertrain components (transmissions, fuel injectors, exhaust systems) face revenue risk as EV share grows. This affects not just automakers but a large ecosystem of parts suppliers.

**Building electrification**: Natural gas appliance manufacturers, gas utilities, and gas infrastructure face long-term demand erosion as heat pumps achieve cost parity and electrification policies spread (California has banned new gas furnaces in new construction; several other states are following).

**Renewable energy displacement**: New solar and wind power is now cheaper than new gas power in most of the world. Coal power plants in competitive markets are being retired early. Gas power plants with 20-30 year lifespans may face premature stranding.

### Market and Reputational Risk

**Consumer and customer pressure**: B2B buyers — particularly large retailers, consumer goods companies, and automakers — are increasingly requiring suppliers to report and reduce emissions. A supplier that cannot produce credible climate data risks losing preferred supplier status.

**Cost of capital**: Institutional investors with net zero commitments are reducing exposure to high-carbon sectors, increasing the cost of equity and debt for those sectors.

**Greenwashing liability**: Climate-related claims in marketing, investor communications, and product labeling are being scrutinized by regulators. The EU Green Claims Directive requires substantiation of environmental claims. The SEC has climate disclosure rules affecting marketing claims for registered investment products. Class action litigation against misleading sustainability claims is increasing.

## Nature and Water Risk in Supply Chains

Nature risk — the risk that business operations depend on, or damage, natural systems — is an emerging but increasingly important category. It extends beyond climate to include biodiversity, deforestation, ocean health, soil quality, and freshwater availability.

**Why it matters for investors**: The Taskforce on Nature-related Financial Disclosures (TNFD) — launched in 2023 — is creating reporting expectations analogous to TCFD but for nature. Major financial institutions are beginning to integrate nature risk into due diligence.

### Sectors Most Exposed

| Sector | Primary nature dependency | Key risk |
|--------|--------------------------|----------|
| Food and beverages | Freshwater, soil health, pollinators, fish stocks | Input supply disruption, regulatory restriction |
| Textiles and apparel | Freshwater (cotton, dyeing), land (fiber crops) | Water scarcity, land degradation |
| Pharmaceuticals | Biodiversity (active compounds, bioprospecting) | Supply chain for natural compounds |
| Timber and paper | Forest health, soil, water | Forest degradation regulations, certification requirements |
| Construction materials | Aggregate, sand, quarried stone | Increasing scarcity in some regions |
| Mining | Land use, freshwater contamination | Regulatory tightening, community opposition |

### Key Data Sources for Nature and Water Risk

**Integrated Biodiversity Assessment Tool (IBAT — ibat-alliance.org)**: Maps species, ecosystems, and protected areas against a proposed project location. Originally built for extractive industries managing biodiversity permits; now used by investors for screening acquisitions. Paid subscription, though basic queries are available.

**WRI Aqueduct (aqueduct.wri.org)**: Global water risk mapping — water stress, drought, riverine flooding, coastal flooding. Free. Identifies whether operations or suppliers are in severely water-stressed basins (a score of 3+ out of 5 warrants attention).

**Global Forest Watch (globalforestwatch.org)**: Tracks deforestation in near-real-time using satellite imagery. Free. Useful for screening agricultural commodity supply chains (soy, palm oil, beef, cocoa) for deforestation exposure.

**CDP Forests (cdp.net)**: Company-reported data on deforestation risk in supply chains. Covers forest-risk commodities (timber, soy, palm oil, beef, leather, rubber). Publicly available for disclosed companies.

**Deforestation-free commitments**: Many large consumer goods companies (Unilever, Nestlé, Walmart) have made deforestation-free supply chain commitments. Suppliers to these companies face audit requirements. The EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR) requires due diligence on soy, beef, palm oil, coffee, cocoa, wood, rubber, and derived products imported to the EU — enforcement begins in 2025.

## Green Bonds and Labeled Instruments

Green bonds, sustainability-linked bonds, and green loans have grown into a $3+ trillion market. Not all are equivalent. Assessing the credibility of labeled instruments requires looking beyond the label.

### Green Bond Principles

The **International Capital Market Association (ICMA) Green Bond Principles** (icmagroup.org) are the market-standard voluntary framework. They require:
1. **Use of proceeds**: Proceeds must be used for defined green projects (renewable energy, energy efficiency, clean transportation, green buildings, sustainable water management, etc.)
2. **Project evaluation and selection**: Issuer must disclose how green projects are selected and what environmental objectives they meet
3. **Management of proceeds**: Proceeds must be tracked in a dedicated account or tracked with a formal allocation approach
4. **Reporting**: Annual reporting on how proceeds are allocated and (ideally) on environmental outcomes

**What to check when evaluating a green bond**:
- Does the issuer have an independent **second-party opinion (SPO)** from a recognized verifier (Sustainalytics, ISS, CICERO, S&P, Moody's, V.E)? An SPO is not required but is now near-standard for credible issuances.
- Is the framework aligned with a recognized taxonomy? EU Taxonomy alignment is the most rigorous standard; ICMA GBP alignment is the market floor.
- Are projects additive, or would they have been funded anyway without the green label?
- Is there outcome reporting, not just allocation reporting? Knowing that $500 million was allocated to "renewable energy projects" is less useful than knowing the installed capacity and estimated emissions reduction.

**Sustainability-linked bonds (SLBs)**: Unlike green bonds (use of proceeds), SLBs tie the coupon rate to the issuer achieving specific sustainability performance targets (SPTs). If targets are missed, the coupon steps up (typically 25-50 basis points). Red flags: weak baseline targets, targets the issuer was on track to hit anyway, targets that don't represent material decarbonization relative to the business.

**Greenwashing red flags**:
- No independent verification or second-party opinion
- Use-of-proceeds categories so broad as to be nearly unlimited (e.g., "general corporate purposes with a sustainability focus")
- No outcome reporting — only allocation reporting
- Sustainability-linked bonds with targets below business-as-usual trajectory

## Portfolio-Level vs. Asset-Level Assessment

### Asset-Level Assessment

For a specific property, project, or company, the goal is to quantify material climate risks and opportunities:
- What physical hazards affect this specific asset?
- What is the regulatory carbon price exposure?
- Is the core business model at risk from technology displacement?
- What is the insurance status and trajectory?

Asset-level assessment is most appropriate for: direct real estate investment, infrastructure acquisition, project finance, private equity, or any transaction where you control the investment terms.

### Portfolio-Level Assessment

For a portfolio of many holdings (public equities, a diversified real estate portfolio, a fixed income portfolio), asset-level assessment is impractical. Portfolio-level climate risk assessment aggregates risk at the portfolio level using sector-based and company-level data.

**Portfolio carbon footprint**: Total financed emissions relative to portfolio value, expressed in tonnes CO2e per million dollars invested. The **Partnership for Carbon Accounting Financials (PCAF)** standard (carbonaccountingfinancials.com) provides methodology for calculating financed emissions. Being adopted by financial institutions globally.

**Weighted average carbon intensity (WACI)**: Emissions per unit of revenue, weighted by portfolio weight. The TCFD recommends WACI as a metric for equity and corporate bond portfolios.

**Portfolio temperature alignment**: Some data providers (MSCI, Carbon Delta) compute a "portfolio implied temperature rise" — the global temperature trajectory consistent with companies in the portfolio achieving their stated targets. A portfolio "aligned to 1.8°C" means its companies' combined targets are consistent with limiting warming to 1.8°C.

## What Institutional Investors Now Require

### Pension Funds

The largest pension funds (CalPERS, CalSTRS, APG, GPIF, and others) have made public net zero commitments and implement them through screening, engagement, and divestment.

**What they expect from investees**:
- Disclosure aligned with TCFD or ISSB S1/S2
- Scope 1 and scope 2 emissions data (scope 3 increasingly expected for high-impact sectors)
- A credible transition plan with near-term (2030) and long-term (2050) targets
- Board-level oversight of climate risk
- Scenario analysis showing how business strategy performs under different climate futures

**What they will act on**: Proxy votes against directors at companies not meeting baseline disclosure standards; engagement letters to management; in some cases divestment from persistent laggards.

### Insurance Companies as Investors

Insurance companies face both sides: as underwriters of physical assets, and as large institutional investors. They are conducting climate stress testing of investment portfolios under requirements from regulators in the EU, UK, and increasingly the US.

### Regulatory Landscape (As of 2024-2025)

**ISSB S2**: The International Sustainability Standards Board's climate disclosure standard. Being adopted as mandatory by UK, Australia, Canada, Singapore, Japan, and others. The most rigorous mandatory climate reporting framework outside the EU.

**EU Taxonomy and SFDR**: The Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation (SFDR) requires EU fund managers to classify products as Article 6 (no sustainability claim), Article 8 (promotes environmental or social characteristics), or Article 9 (sustainable investment as objective). Creates investor pull for climate data from portfolio companies.

**SEC Climate Disclosure Rule**: The US Securities and Exchange Commission finalized a climate disclosure rule in 2024 requiring large public companies to disclose material climate-related risks and greenhouse gas emissions. Litigation has delayed implementation, but the regulatory direction is clear.

**TCFD**: The Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures framework — Governance, Strategy, Risk Management, Metrics and Targets — remains the foundation for most climate reporting. Now being superseded by ISSB S2 but the four pillars are embedded in most voluntary and mandatory reporting frameworks.

## Investment Due Diligence: Sustainability Risk Section

For acquisitions, project finance, or investment reports, the sustainability risk section should address:

### Executive Summary (3-5 bullets)

Lead with the most material risks. Be specific:
- "The target asset's primary facility is in FEMA Zone AE. Current flood insurance is available but premium has increased 60% in two years following NFIP Risk Rating 2.0 repricing. First Street Foundation flood score: 8/10."
- "The company derives 65% of revenue from internal combustion engine powertrain components. No disclosed plan for transition to EV applications."
- "The property is in a CAL FIRE Very High Fire Hazard Severity Zone. Standard homeowner's insurance is no longer available through private carriers; current policy is California FAIR Plan."

### Physical Risk Section

- Asset location screen results for each major facility (flood zone, wildfire score, water stress, heat days)
- First Street Foundation or equivalent score for each location
- Key hazards and time horizons (current, 10-year, 30-year)
- Insurance status, recent premium trajectory, and non-renewal history
- Resilience measures in place or planned

### Transition Risk Section

- Sector transition risk profile (high/medium/low; which risks apply)
- Carbon price exposure estimate (methodology disclosed; carbon price assumption stated)
- Regulatory risk assessment (which regulations are material, timeline)
- Technology disruption risk (is the core business model at risk from cleaner alternatives?)

### Nature and Water Risk

- Water dependence assessment: does the business rely on large volumes of freshwater in a stressed basin? (WRI Aqueduct score)
- Supply chain deforestation exposure: does the company source forest-risk commodities (soy, palm oil, beef, cocoa, timber)?
- Regulatory exposure: is the company or its suppliers subject to EUDR, deforestation-free procurement policies, or biodiversity permit requirements?

### Management Assessment

- Does management have a credible climate strategy? Published targets?
- What is the governance structure? Board-level oversight?
- Has progress been reported publicly?
- Are targets validated by a third party (SBTi or equivalent)?

### Red Flags: Immediate Attention Required

- No climate disclosure, no targets, no strategy
- Heavy fossil fuel exposure without a credible transition plan
- Physical assets in high-risk locations with no insurance or mitigation plan
- Insurance non-renewals or significant recent premium increases
- Sustainability claims in marketing or investor materials not supported by data
- Sustainability-linked financing with targets below business-as-usual trajectory

## Quick Reference Checklist

**Physical risk — data to pull**:
- [ ] Look up each major facility at riskfactor.com — note flood, wildfire, and heat scores; check 30-year projection
- [ ] Check FEMA flood zone at msc.fema.gov for US properties
- [ ] Check CAL FIRE FHSZ designation for California properties at osfm.fire.ca.gov
- [ ] Check water stress for manufacturing, agricultural, or data center assets at aqueduct.wri.org
- [ ] For coastal assets: check NOAA Sea Level Rise Viewer at coast.noaa.gov/slr
- [ ] Check insurance status: is the property insurable through standard carriers? Has premium increased >20% in 2 years?

**Transition risk — questions to ask**:
- [ ] What is the company's carbon footprint (scope 1 and 2)? Has it been third-party verified?
- [ ] Is the company operating in a jurisdiction with a carbon pricing system? (see carbonpricingdashboard.worldbank.org)
- [ ] What share of revenue is at risk from technology displacement (EV transition, electrification, renewable energy)?
- [ ] Does the company have disclosed climate targets? Are they validated by SBTi or an equivalent?

**Nature and supply chain risk**:
- [ ] Does the business source forest-risk commodities? Check CDP Forests disclosure at cdp.net
- [ ] Is the business water-intensive and operating in a stressed basin? Check WRI Aqueduct
- [ ] Is the business subject to EUDR or deforestation-free procurement requirements?

**For real estate specifically**:
- [ ] Is the property in a FEMA Zone AE or VE, or First Street score 7+?
- [ ] Can the property obtain standard insurance at reasonable cost?
- [ ] Is the property in a jurisdiction with mandatory minimum energy performance standards?
- [ ] What is the Energy Performance Certificate rating (EU/UK) or ENERGY STAR score?

**Green bonds and labeled instruments**:
- [ ] Is there an independent second-party opinion from a recognized verifier?
- [ ] Are use-of-proceeds categories clearly defined and limited to credible green activities?
- [ ] Is there outcome reporting, not just allocation reporting?
- [ ] For sustainability-linked bonds: are targets material relative to business-as-usual trajectory?

**Institutional reporting baseline**:
- [ ] Does the company disclose under TCFD or ISSB S2?
- [ ] Is scope 1 and scope 2 data publicly available and verified?
- [ ] Is there a board-level climate governance structure?
